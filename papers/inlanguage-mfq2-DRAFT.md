# The Language Carries the Culture, the Prompt Carries the Caricature: In-Language Administration Dissociates Two Sources of Cultural Behavior in Language Models

## Summary

When a language model is asked to answer a moral questionnaire "as a typical person from Egypt," is the profile it produces drawn from what the model can express about Egyptians, or from a stereotype the instruction activates? Our earlier society-framing study could not separate the two, because the human norms it compared against were collected in local languages while the models answered in English. Here we close that gap. We administered the official Arabic, Japanese, and Farsi translations of the Moral Foundations Questionnaire-2 (MFQ-2) to the same 11-model panel, in two conditions per language: framed (answer as a typical person from Egypt, Japan, or Iran, with the framing instruction given in that language) and neutral (no system prompt, items in the target language). The neutral in-language condition is the new instrument: it separates answering in Arabic from answering as an Egyptian. Three results. First, the caricature survives translation: framed-as-Egypt in Arabic is statistically indistinguishable from the English-framed profile, with any difference bounded within 0.04 points (per-model paired difference -0.006, 95% CI [-0.038, +0.025]), and both overshoot the human anchor by +0.34 (95% CI [+0.25, +0.43]); the panel's collapse into a shared portrait (between-model SD 0.15) reproduces in every framed condition. Second, the unframed default is language-indexed, but what the language carries varies enormously by language: Japanese neutral is indistinguishable from the measured Japanese population mean (panel 2.66 against 2.65; displacement +0.01, 95% CI [-0.16, +0.19]), Arabic neutral moves a third of a point toward binding (+0.33 over the English default, 95% CI [+0.21, +0.46]) but covers only a fraction of the distance to the Egyptian mean, and Farsi neutral is indistinguishable from the English default (difference +0.02, 95% CI [-0.15, +0.17]). Third, the framing template is far more uniform than the populations are: framed-as-Iran lands at 4.36 against a measured Iranian mean of 3.33, an overshoot of +1.03 (95% CI [+0.90, +1.16], every model in the panel overshooting), placing framed Iran 0.25 points from framed Egypt while the two measured populations sit 0.94 points apart. The two layers dissociate cleanly. For Japan, the panel produces the population's profile under mere in-language administration and the framing instruction moves it away from that profile by 0.81 points. A culturally framed deployment can be less accurate than an unframed one in the local language, even when the framed and unframed behavior come from the same model.

## Method

**Instruments.** The MFQ-2 (Atari, Haidt, Graham, Koleva, Stevens & Dehghani, 2023): 36 items, six foundations (Care, Equality, Proportionality, Loyalty, Authority, Purity), rated 1 to 5. We used the official translations distributed in the authors' OSF materials (osf.io/srtxn): Arabic, Japanese, and Farsi, plus English for the baseline runs. Item-to-foundation keying follows the official cycle and was independently confirmed by the scoring key included in the Farsi translation file. Foundation scores are means of their six items; the binding composite is the mean of Loyalty, Authority, and Purity.

**Panel and protocol.** The same 11 models as the pilot and the society-framing run (Anthropic, OpenAI, xAI, Mistral, and Together-hosted DeepSeek, MiniMax, Inkling, Kimi, Llama; two vendors excluded earlier for rate-limit policies). Each model completed each condition five times with item order randomized per iteration, one questionnaire per call, ratings returned as JSON. 329 of 330 cells returned parseable ratings; the one failing cell (a single kimi iteration) leaves that model-condition resting on four iterations.

**Conditions.** Per language: framed, a system prompt instructing the model to answer as a typical, ordinary person living in the country (Egypt for Arabic, Japan for Japanese, Iran for Farsi), written in the target language; and neutral, no system prompt, items and scale anchors in the target language. The framing-instruction translations are ours (AI-assisted, recorded verbatim in every output cell); the item and anchor wording is the official translation throughout. English framed and neutral baselines come from the prior society-framing run under the identical protocol.

**Human anchors.** Egypt and Japan: national means from Atari et al. (2023, Study 2, Table 7), collected in-country in local languages. Iran: Hazrati, Nejat and Daneshi (2025), an independent validation of the Persian MFQ-2 (Atari's translation with minor edits) in two Iranian samples; we anchor on the larger replication sample (N = 989) and rescale their 0-4 administration to 1-5 by a +1 shift. This anchor skews young, educated, online, and about 70 percent female, and was collected in 2023-24; we treat it as representing connected young Iranians and flag every Iran comparison accordingly.

**Statistical approach.** The independent unit is the model: eleven clusters, so every analysis aggregates each model's iterations first and tests at the model level. Panel means carry 95 percent bootstrap confidence intervals (resampling models, 100,000 iterations, seed 20260723). Paired and one-sample comparisons use exact sign-flip permutation tests on per-model differences (all 2,048 sign patterns enumerated; minimum attainable two-sided p is 0.001). Eight comparisons form one declared family, Holm-corrected; because no analysis was pre-registered, all eight are exploratory, and we label them so. Human anchors are treated as known constants; their standard errors (N of 989 to several thousand per country) are an order of magnitude smaller than the effects at issue. Null results are stated as bounded intervals, never as demonstrated absence. The audit script (validity/audit_inlanguage.py) reproduces every inferential number deterministically, and its panel means were reconciled against the descriptive analysis before any resampling was trusted.

## Results

Binding composite by condition (panel mean across 11 models; between-model SD in parentheses):

| country | human anchor | EN framed | in-language framed | in-language neutral | EN neutral |
|---|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|
| Egypt (Arabic) | 4.27 | 4.61 (0.15) | 4.61 (0.15) | 3.03 (0.46) | 2.71 (0.34) |
| Japan (Japanese) | 2.65 | 3.67 (0.29) | 3.46 (0.16) | 2.66 (0.29) | 2.71 (0.34) |
| Iran (Farsi) | 3.33 | not run | 4.36 (0.22) | 2.72 (0.50) | 2.71 (0.34) |

**The caricature survives translation.** Egypt's framed overshoot is +0.34 in English and +0.34 in Arabic (vs anchor: +0.34, 95% CI [+0.25, +0.43], Holm-adjusted p = .008); the per-model paired English-vs-Arabic difference is -0.006 with a 95% CI of [-0.038, +0.025], so any language effect on the framed Egyptian profile is smaller than 0.04 points. Since the English cells were collected three days before the Arabic cells, this bound doubles as a stability check on the panel across collection dates. Japan's overshoot shrinks from +1.02 to +0.81 under Japanese administration (paired difference +0.21, 95% CI [+0.08, +0.36], Holm-adjusted p = .039) but persists. In every framed condition the panel converges into a shared portrait (between-model SD 0.15 to 0.22, at or below the panel's neutral disagreement of 0.34; descriptive, no test).

**The default is language-indexed, and unevenly.** Japanese neutral (2.66, 95% CI [2.50, 2.84]) is indistinguishable from the measured Japanese mean of 2.65: the displacement is +0.01 with a 95% CI of [-0.16, +0.19], so any deviation from the population mean is bounded within a fifth of a scale point, against a framed displacement four times that bound. Arabic neutral sits +0.33 above the English default (95% CI [+0.21, +0.46], Holm-adjusted p = .008) and shows the panel's widest disagreement (SD 0.46; descriptive), carrying real but partial binding signal: the point estimate covers roughly a quarter of the distance from the English default to the Egyptian mean. Farsi neutral is indistinguishable from the English default (+0.02, 95% CI [-0.15, +0.17]): any signal the language carries is bounded below 0.17 points. The language of administration can carry a population's profile completely, partially, or not detectably at all.

**The framing template is far more uniform than the populations.** Framed-as-Iran (4.36, 95% CI [4.23, 4.49]) lands 0.25 points from framed-as-Egypt (4.61, 95% CI [4.52, 4.70]) although the measured populations sit 0.94 points apart (3.33 vs 4.27). The Iran overshoot is +1.03 (95% CI [+0.90, +1.16], Holm-adjusted p = .008); every one of the eleven models overshoots, and the leave-one-model-out panel overshoot never drops below +0.99. Per foundation, the error concentrates where a traditional-society template predicts: Authority 4.48 against a measured 3.05, Loyalty 4.45 against 3.63, Purity 4.14 against 3.32, while Equality is accurate to two hundredths (2.65 against 2.67). The models do not miss Iran randomly; they render it as a generic high-binding religious society, which the measured population, at least its young connected stratum, is not. One caveat on the Iran-Egypt comparison: Iran has no English-framed arm, so the template's language-invariance is established for Egypt and inferred for Iran.

**The dissociation.** Framing moves every panel strongly relative to its in-language neutral (Arabic +1.58, Japanese +0.80 with 95% CI [+0.64, +0.96] and Holm-adjusted p = .008, Farsi +1.64) regardless of how much population signal the language carried. For Japan the two layers pull in opposite directions: in-language administration alone places the panel within 0.19 points of the population mean at 95 percent confidence, and the framing instruction moves it +0.80 away. The distortion documented in the society-framing study is therefore not an absence of population-consistent behavior in the models; under Japanese administration the panel produces that behavior. The framing instruction overrides it with a template.

## Limitations

Eleven models is a panel, not a sample; inferential statements are about this panel, between-model SDs characterize this panel only, and nothing here was pre-registered, so every test is exploratory however small its corrected p. We ran one instrument, three languages, and one framed country per language; whether Arabic neutral would track Gulf, Levantine, or North African means differently is unmeasured, and the Arabic language cannot be assigned to a single national population at all, which may itself explain its partial carry. Human anchors are single studies per country, and the Iran anchor is a convenience sample from a different research group than the Egypt and Japan anchors, collected on a shifted response scale that we rescaled linearly; its youth-and-online skew means the true Iranian population mean is plausibly higher than 3.33, which would shrink the Iran overshoot, though a 1.4-point Authority error does not plausibly reduce to sampling. The framing-instruction translations are ours, not validated; a mistranslated nuance could inflate framed-condition artifacts, though the English-Arabic identity on Egypt argues against this. Foundation-level SDs for the Iranian sample are not published, so Iran overshoots are reported in raw scale points, not standardized units. Models have plausibly seen the MFQ-2 items and published norms in training; as in the society-framing study, results should be read as steerability and behavioral repertoire, not cultural understanding. The neutral Japanese result in particular is consistent with both a genuinely internalized Japanese population profile and memorized questionnaire norms surfacing under Japanese text; distinguishing these requires an instrument the models have not seen, which is what the Reasoner program exists to build.

## What this changes

Cultural adaptation by prompt and cultural knowledge by language are separable behaviors, and they can disagree. Evaluations of "cultural alignment" that only test framed behavior measure the template; evaluations that only test the default in English miss what the model produces under local-language administration entirely. Deployment follows the same logic: an assistant serving a population in its own language may sit closer to that population unframed than the same assistant explicitly instructed to adopt the culture. The confirmatory Reasoner design gains a condition: in-language administration alongside neutral and framed, on scenarios that carry no published norms to memorize.
